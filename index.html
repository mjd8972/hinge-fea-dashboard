Design iterations — what R1, R2 and R3 represent
R1 — Baseline
Original hinge design
The existing hinge joint as used on the Top Spin ride. Two clevis-style lug arms with a smooth circular cross-section, a large D-shaped backplate with 20 bolt holes, and a standard clevis pin through both lug eyes. This is the baseline against which all improvements are measured. All three analyses — single body static structural, assembly fatigue, and modal — were performed on R1 first to establish reference values.
R2 — Recommended
Rib-reinforced design
The first design iteration. Four ribs added in total — two ribs on each of the two lug eyes — running along the outer face of each lug arm in the primary bending direction. The backplate, bolt hole pattern, and clevis pin remain identical to R1. The ribs increase bending stiffness of the lug arms without modifying the pin-lug contact geometry, preserving the load transfer interface while reducing stress and deformation significantly.
R3 — Investigated
Fundamentally redesigned lug geometry
The second design iteration. Rather than adding ribs, the lug eye geometry itself was redesigned — incorporating a triangular gusseted backing plate integrated into the lug arm structure, creating a stiffer and more material-efficient lug form. The backplate and bolt pattern again remain identical. R3 showed the best single-body static performance of all three designs but was found to cause a critical pin fatigue failure mode when analysed at assembly level.

Design life calculation — complete derivation
1
Ride duration and rotations: Each Top Spin ride lasts 2 minutes. The cabin rotates at 15 RPM throughout. Rotations per ride = 15 × 2 = 30 rotations per ride. Each complete rotation subjects the hinge to one full stress reversal — tension on one half rotation, compression on the other.
2
Realistic rides per hour: Ride duration = 2 min. Passenger boarding + safety harness check + deboarding for 20 passengers = approximately 4 min turnaround. Total per ride = 6 min. Rides per hour = 60 ÷ 6 = 10 rides/hour. Note: 30 rides/hour is physically impossible as it ignores all boarding and safety procedures.
3
Cycles per hour: 10 rides × 30 rotations/ride = 300 stress cycles/hour
4
Cycles per day: 300 cycles/hr × 8 operating hours/day = 2,400 cycles/day
5
Cycles per year: 2,400 × 240 operating days/year = 576,000 cycles/year. 240 days accounts for seasonal operation, scheduled maintenance shutdowns, and off-season closure typical of amusement parks.
6
Total design life: 576,000 × 20 years = 11,520,000 = 1.152 × 10⁷ cycles
N_design = 10 rides/hr × 30 cycles/ride × 8 hr/day × 240 days/yr × 20 yr
N_design = 1.152 × 10⁷ cycles

Since N_design = 1.152×10⁷ > 1×10⁶ → endurance limit zone governs
Infinite life criterion applies — stress must stay below corrected Se at all points
Marin equation — corrected endurance limit step by step

The laboratory endurance limit Se' comes from rotating-bending tests on perfectly polished 8mm steel rods. Your actual hinge is a large machined part carrying 20 passengers on a public ride. Each Marin factor corrects for one real-world condition that the lab test does not represent:

Se' = 0.5 × Sᵤₜ = 0.5 × 440 = 220 MPa (laboratory specimen baseline)

Se = ka × kb × kc × kd × ke × Se'

ka = 0.72 (surface finish — machined steel, Sᵤₜ = 440 MPa, Shigley's Table 6-2)
kb = 0.85 (size factor — part cross-section > 50mm)
kc = 1.00 (load type — von-Mises handles combined loading directly)
kd = 1.00 (temperature — ambient operation, below 150°C threshold)
ke = 0.868 (reliability — 95% required for public safety ride)

Se = 0.72 × 0.85 × 1.00 × 1.00 × 0.868 × 220
Se = 0.531 × 220 = 116.8 MPa ← corrected endurance limit before stress concentration

Kf (textbook) = 1.4 for machined through-holes under bending loading
Kf_ANSYS = 1 ÷ 1.4 = 0.714 (ANSYS uses strength reduction, not stress multiplier — equivalent result)

Se_final = 116.8 × 0.714 = 83.4 MPa ← final effective endurance limit used in fatigue tool
ka
Surface finish (0.72): Machined surfaces have microscopic tool marks that act as crack initiation sites. The polished lab specimen has none. For Sᵤₜ = 440 MPa machined finish, ka = 0.72 from Shigley's empirical correlation. This is the single largest penalty factor.
kb
Size factor (0.85): Larger parts contain more volume, hence statistically greater probability of a critical microdefect being present in the highest-stressed region. The lab rod is 8mm; your hinge lug is 50–80mm. kb = 0.85 for parts over 50mm cross section.
ke
Reliability factor (0.868): The lab Se' value represents 50% reliability — meaning statistically half the specimens would fail at this stress. For a public amusement ride carrying 20 passengers, 50% is unacceptable. 95% reliability requires ke = 0.868, derived from the standard deviation of fatigue data scatter for steel.
Kf
Stress concentration (Kf_ANSYS = 0.714): The lug through-holes are geometric stress concentrators. Textbook Kf = 1.4 means the actual stress at the hole edge is 1.4× the nominal stress. ANSYS applies this as a strength reduction factor (1/1.4 = 0.714) rather than a stress amplifier — mathematically identical, just inverted definition. Applied after all other Marin factors.
Failure criterion — why von-Mises
Ductile material · Distortion energy theory
Von-Mises equivalent stress
AISI 1018 is a ductile metal. For ductile materials, yielding begins when the distortion energy per unit volume equals the distortion energy at the tensile yield point — not when any single stress component reaches yield. Von-Mises collapses all six stress tensor components into one equivalent scalar directly comparable to Sᵧ:
σ_vm = √( ½[(σ₁−σ₂)² + (σ₂−σ₃)² + (σ₃−σ₁)²] )

Failure criterion: σ_vm ≥ Sᵧ = 370 MPa
All three designs: max σ_vm < 60 MPa → well within safe range
vs Principal
Maximum principal stress criterion is for brittle materials (cast iron, ceramics, glass) that fracture before yielding. AISI 1018 yields — using principal stress criterion would give wrong, non-conservative predictions for this material.
vs Tresca
Tresca (maximum shear stress) is also valid for ductile metals but is conservatively 15% below von-Mises — it underestimates strength. Von-Mises is more accurate, is the industry standard for steel FEA, and is what ANSYS uses as its default equivalent stress output.
Hinge case
The hinge lug experiences simultaneous bending stress, transverse shear, and contact pressure at the pin interface. Von-Mises naturally handles this multi-axial stress state in a single number — no manual stress combination required.
Mean stress theory — why Goodman
Fatigue mean stress correction · Conservative · Industry standard
Goodman line
The S-N curve is measured at R = −1 (zero mean stress). When a tensile mean stress exists alongside cyclic alternating stress, fatigue life reduces — tensile mean stress helps cracks propagate. Three theories describe this reduction:
Goodman: (σa / Se) + (σm / Sᵤₜ) = 1 ← straight line to Sᵤₜ — conservative
Gerber: (σa / Se) + (σm / Sᵤₜ)² = 1 ← parabola — less conservative, closer to test data
Soderberg: (σa / Se) + (σm / Sᵧ) = 1 ← most conservative, often too cautious for steel

For fully reversed loading (our case): σm = 0
Goodman simplifies to: σa ≤ Se = 83.4 MPa
R1 assembly σa = 57.9 MPa < 83.4 MPa → Safe ✓
R2 assembly σa = 33.2 MPa < 83.4 MPa → Safe ✓
Goodman is selected because it is the standard for safety-critical engineering. It is conservative without being as overly conservative as Soderberg. For a public ride carrying 20 passengers — erring on the safe side is mandatory. Goodman is also the criterion ANSYS implements by name in its Fatigue Tool mean stress correction options.
Loading type — why fully reversed (R = −1)
Physical loading justification
Continuous rotation → equal tension and compression every cycle
The Top Spin cabin rotates continuously at 15 RPM. The hinge joint is the pivot point. As the cabin swings one direction the hinge lug is pulled in tension. Half a rotation later it is pushed in compression. The stress cycles perfectly and symmetrically between +σ and −σ every single rotation:
R = σ_min / σ_max = −57.9 / +57.9 = −1.0 (fully reversed)

σ_mean = (σ_max + σ_min) / 2 = (57.9 + (−57.9)) / 2 = 0 MPa
σ_amplitude = (σ_max − σ_min) / 2 = 57.9 MPa (R1 assembly value)

Stress history: +57.9 → 0 → −57.9 → 0 → +57.9 → ... (sine wave, 0.25 Hz)
R = −1 is the most damaging possible loading type because the material is alternately stretched and compressed with equal magnitude every cycle — no rest, no recovery. This is therefore the most conservative and physically correct assumption for a continuously rotating pivot mechanism.

Final recommendation — R2 rib-reinforced design is optimal
Among the three hinge designs analysed for the Top Spin ride operating at 15 RPM over a 20-year design life of 1.152×10⁷ cycles, R2 — the rib-reinforced design — is the recommended iteration. It achieves the lowest assembly stress (33.216 MPa — 42.6% reduction from R1 baseline), the highest fatigue safety factor (2.4966 — 74% improvement over R1), the lowest deformation (0.02467 mm — 57% reduction), and confirmed infinite fatigue life exceeding 1×10⁸ cycles under fully reversed Goodman loading. Its first natural frequency of 393.51 Hz provides a resonance separation ratio of 1,574 — confirming complete dynamic safety for the operating regime. R3 requires further geometric refinement at the lug-pin interface before it can be considered a viable alternative despite its superior single-body static performance.
Key engineering insights from this project
Critical finding
R3 exposes the value of assembly analysis
Single-body analysis predicted R3 as best — lowest stress (40.5 MPa), highest static safety factor (9.13). Assembly-level fatigue revealed minimum life of only 1×10⁶ cycles at the clevis pin — 11.5× below the 1.152×10⁷ cycle requirement. R3's redesigned triangular gusseted lug geometry is too stiff at the pin-lug interface. This stiffness creates edge loading — contact pressure concentrates at the two lug edges where the pin exits rather than distributing along the contact length. The pin fails in fatigue long before the hinge body does. This phenomenon — a geometrically improved component causing failure in its connected component — is invisible in single-body analysis and only reveals itself through assembly-level frictional contact simulation.
Design principle
Why ribs beat redesign for pin-joint applications
R2 adds ribs along the lug arm bending load path without touching the lug eye geometry at the pin interface. The pin-lug contact zone remains identical to R1 — load transfer at the interface is unchanged. Result: all the stiffness benefit of ribs with none of the contact penalty. R3 redesigns the lug shape itself, changing the contact geometry and causing edge loading. The engineering principle revealed: in a pin joint, improve the structural path away from the contact zone — do not modify the contact geometry itself unless you have done assembly-level contact analysis to verify the effect on the connected component.
Modal insight
Closely spaced modes — a structural fingerprint
R1 Modes 1 and 2 are only 11.7 Hz apart (365.21 and 376.91 Hz). This indicates nearly equal bending stiffness in two perpendicular planes — the circular lug arm cross-section flexes equally in all directions. When R2 adds directional ribs, Mode 2 jumps to 626 Hz — a 66% increase — while Mode 1 rises only 7.7%. The ribs selectively stiffen bending in the primary loading plane. This dramatic separation of two previously closely spaced modes is a structural fingerprint that only modal analysis reveals, and it quantitatively confirms that the ribs are performing exactly their intended function.
Validation insight
Modal analysis validates the fatigue model
The fatigue analysis assumed a quasi-static 12,500 N load amplitude without dynamic amplification. This assumption is valid only if the operating excitation frequency is far from structural natural frequencies — otherwise resonance amplifies the actual stress above the nominal value, making fatigue predictions non-conservative. The minimum separation ratio of 1,461 for R1 and 1,574 for R2 — both more than 1,000× the engineering safety threshold of 1.5 — conclusively validates this assumption. No dynamic amplification occurs. The fatigue life calculations are conservative and accurate for the intended operating conditions.

About these charts
These three supplementary charts are derived entirely from ANSYS results already presented in this dashboard — no additional analysis was required. They provide alternative visual framings of the same data: the Goodman diagram shows safety margins graphically on the failure boundary, the performance index quantifies structural efficiency per unit mass, and the S-N curve contextualises each design's operating stress against the material fatigue curve. Chart 3 — Modified Goodman diagram with design operating points
What this shows
The Modified Goodman diagram is the standard graphical tool for fatigue safety assessment. The Goodman line connects the corrected endurance limit Se = 83.4 MPa on the vertical axis to the ultimate tensile strength Sut = 440 MPa on the horizontal axis. Any operating point below this line achieves infinite fatigue life. The Gerber parabola and Soderberg line are shown for comparison — Goodman was selected as it is conservative without being overly pessimistic. Since all loading is fully reversed (R = −1, zero mean stress), all three design operating points plot on the vertical axis at σm = 0. The horizontal distance from a point to the Goodman line represents the available overload margin.
Goodman (selected) Gerber parabola Soderberg line R1 — 57.9 MPa R2 — 33.2 MPa (recommended) R3 pin — ~95 MPa (fails)
Reading the diagram: R1 at 57.9 MPa and R2 at 33.2 MPa both sit comfortably below the Goodman line — confirmed infinite fatigue life. R2 has nearly twice the distance below the line compared to R1, explaining its higher fatigue safety factor of 2.497 vs 1.433. The R3 clevis pin operating point at ~95 MPa sits above the corrected endurance limit of 83.4 MPa — this is the graphical confirmation of why ANSYS reports only 1×10⁶ cycles at the pin. Goodman correctly predicts finite life when σa > Se.
Chart 4 — Structural performance index (fatigue safety factor per unit mass)
What this shows
The performance index — fatigue safety factor divided by hinge mass — quantifies structural efficiency. A higher index means more fatigue resistance is achieved per kilogram of material. This metric is commonly used in engineering design optimisation to distinguish between designs that improve performance through mass addition versus those that improve through intelligent geometry. R2's index is 71.5% higher than R1, demonstrating that the ribs deliver disproportionately large structural benefit relative to their small mass addition. R3's index appears competitive but is misleading — its assembly-level failure at the pin means the hinge body safety factor cannot be realised in practice.

Chart 5 — S-N curve with design operating stresses
What this shows
The S-N (stress-life) curve for AISI 1018 steel as defined in ANSYS Engineering Data, plotted on logarithmic axes. The curve has been corrected by the Fatigue Strength Factor Kf = 0.714 applied in the ANSYS Fatigue Tool, reflecting real-world stress concentration at the hinge lug holes. The flat horizontal plateau beginning at 10⁷ cycles represents the endurance limit Se = 83.4 MPa — below this stress amplitude the material theoretically never fails regardless of cycle count. Each design's assembly-level operating stress is shown as a horizontal dashed line. Where a stress line falls below the plateau, the design achieves infinite life. Where it falls above, finite life applies and the intersection with the curve gives the predicted fatigue life in cycles.
